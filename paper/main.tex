\begin{abstract}
We present a hybrid quantum-classical execution engine for optimal trade execution in high-frequency trading (HFT) environments. Our framework formulates the multi-period, multi-venue order execution problem as a Quadratic Unconstrained Binary Optimization (QUBO) problem with a novel six-term cost function that integrates real-time market microstructure signals---Kyle's lambda, Volume-Synchronized Probability of Informed Trading (VPIN), adverse selection costs, and queue position dynamics. Unlike prior quantum finance approaches that rely on static cost models, our formulation dynamically adapts risk aversion through a dual-timescale Exponentially Weighted Moving Average (EWMA) volatility regime detector, enabling regime-conditioned optimization. We introduce a latency-decoupled architecture that separates sub-millisecond execution decisions (fast path) from quantum/classical optimization (slow path, 100ms--5s) via an asynchronous policy queue, bounding the staleness regret at $O(\sigma\sqrt{T_{\text{solve}}})$. Extensive benchmarks on IBM Quantum hardware and calibrated noise simulators demonstrate that our Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA) implementation achieves approximation ratios of 0.95--1.00 for problems up to 12 qubits, with simulated annealing providing a classical fallback that matches optimal solutions for current problem sizes. Walk-forward backtesting across multiple volatility regimes shows 15--40\% implementation shortfall reduction compared to static VWAP strategies. This work provides the first end-to-end integration of quantum optimization, market microstructure analysis, adaptive risk management, and nanosecond-resolution latency monitoring for algorithmic trade execution.

\keywords{Quantum computing \and QUBO \and QAOA \and Trade execution \and Market microstructure \and High-frequency trading \and Hybrid optimization \and Latency-decoupled architecture}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Optimal trade execution---the problem of liquidating a large position while minimizing market impact and timing risk---remains one of the most significant challenges in quantitative finance~\cite{almgren2001optimal, bertsimas1998optimal}. The seminal Almgren-Chriss framework~\cite{almgren2001optimal} established the theoretical foundation by formulating execution as a mean-variance optimization over a discrete time grid, producing the well-known $\sinh$-trajectory solution:
\begin{equation}
x(t) = X \cdot \frac{\sinh(\kappa(T-t))}{\sinh(\kappa T)}, \quad \kappa = \sqrt{\frac{\lambda \sigma^2}{\eta}}
\label{eq:almgren_chriss}
\end{equation}
where $X$ is total shares, $\lambda$ is risk aversion, $\sigma$ is volatility, and $\eta$ is temporary impact.

However, modern electronic markets present challenges that the classical framework does not address: (i) multi-venue routing across lit exchanges, dark pools, and ECNs with heterogeneous adverse selection profiles; (ii) tick-level microstructure dynamics including informed trading detection and queue priority; and (iii) the combinatorial explosion of the joint time-venue-quantity optimization space.

Recent advances in quantum computing have motivated the application of quantum optimization to financial problems~\cite{orus2019quantum, egger2020quantum}. The Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA)~\cite{farhi2014quantum} and quantum annealing~\cite{kadowaki1998quantum} naturally solve QUBO problems, which can encode combinatorial optimization objectives. Several works have applied QUBO formulations to portfolio optimization~\cite{mugel2022dynamic}, option pricing~\cite{stamatopoulos2020option}, and transaction settlement~\cite{braine2021quantum}.

Despite these advances, no existing work provides an end-to-end system that combines: (1) a microstructure-aware QUBO formulation with real-time market signals, (2) adaptive risk aversion that responds to volatility regime changes, (3) a latency-decoupled architecture suitable for production HFT deployment, and (4) empirical validation on real quantum hardware. This paper fills that gap with the following contributions:

\begin{itemize}
\item A \textbf{six-term HFT QUBO cost function} (Section~\ref{sec:qubo_formulation}) incorporating market impact, timing risk, transaction costs, adverse selection (from Kyle's lambda and VPIN), inventory risk, and cross-venue information leakage.

\item A \textbf{dual-timescale adaptive risk manager} (Section~\ref{sec:adaptive_risk}) that detects volatility regimes (LOW/NORMAL/HIGH/EXTREME) via fast and slow EWMA estimators and dynamically adjusts the QUBO risk aversion parameter $\lambda$.

\item A \textbf{latency-decoupled execution architecture} (Section~\ref{sec:architecture}) with sub-millisecond fast path execution and asynchronous quantum/classical optimization, with formal staleness regret bounds.

\item \textbf{Comprehensive empirical evaluation} (Section~\ref{sec:results}) including QAOA vs.\ simulated annealing comparisons, IBM Quantum hardware benchmarks, noise-aware performance analysis, walk-forward backtesting, and stress scenario testing across 38 publication-quality figures.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Classical Optimal Execution}
The Almgren-Chriss framework~\cite{almgren2001optimal} established the continuous-time mean-variance formulation. Bertsimas and Lo~\cite{bertsimas1998optimal} provided the discrete-time dynamic programming solution. Cartea and Jaimungal~\cite{cartea2015algorithmic} extended the framework to incorporate limit orders and market microstructure. Gu{\'e}ant et al.~\cite{gueant2012optimal} solved the optimal execution problem with stochastic volatility. These classical approaches assume continuous relaxation of the decision space and do not naturally handle the combinatorial venue-routing problem.

\subsection{Quantum Optimization in Finance}
Or{\'u}s et al.~\cite{orus2019quantum} surveyed quantum computing applications in finance. Rosenberg et al.~\cite{rosenberg2016solving} first applied QUBO to portfolio optimization on D-Wave quantum annealers. Mugel et al.~\cite{mugel2022dynamic} demonstrated dynamic portfolio optimization using hybrid quantum-classical methods. Egger et al.~\cite{egger2020quantum} provided a comprehensive framework for quantum computational finance. Brandhofer et al.~\cite{brandhofer2023benchmarking} benchmarked QAOA for combinatorial optimization on near-term hardware.

Notably, no prior work applies QUBO formulations to the \emph{execution} problem (as opposed to portfolio selection), and none incorporates real-time microstructure signals into the quantum optimization objective.

\subsection{Market Microstructure}
Kyle~\cite{kyle1985continuous} introduced the lambda model of price impact from informed trading. Easley et al.~\cite{easley2012flow} developed VPIN as a real-time measure of order flow toxicity. Cartea et al.~\cite{cartea2015algorithmic} provided the modern framework for algorithmic trading with microstructure awareness. Our work is the first to encode these microstructure signals directly into QUBO cost terms.

\section{QUBO Formulation for Trade Execution}
\label{sec:qubo_formulation}

\subsection{Decision Variables}
We define binary decision variables $x_{t,v,k} \in \{0,1\}$ where $t \in \{1,\ldots,T\}$ indexes time slices, $v \in \{1,\ldots,V\}$ indexes venues, and $k \in \{1,\ldots,K\}$ indexes quantity levels $q_k$. The variable $x_{t,v,k} = 1$ indicates executing $q_k$ shares at venue $v$ during time slice $t$.

The total number of binary variables is $n = T \times V \times K$, yielding a QUBO matrix $Q \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$.

\subsection{Objective Function}
The objective minimizes:
\begin{equation}
\min_{\mathbf{x}} \; \mathbf{x}^T Q \mathbf{x} = \sum_{i=1}^{6} w_i \cdot C_i(\mathbf{x}) + \text{Penalties}
\label{eq:qubo_objective}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Term 1: Market Impact Cost}
\begin{equation}
C_1 = \sum_{t} \eta \left(\sum_{v,k} q_k x_{t,v,k}\right)^2
\end{equation}
where $\eta$ is the temporary impact coefficient, reflecting the quadratic price impact of aggregate volume per time slice.

\subsubsection{Term 2: Timing Risk Cost}
\begin{equation}
C_2 = \lambda \sigma^2 \sum_{t} t \cdot \left(\sum_{v,k} q_k x_{t,v,k}\right)^2
\end{equation}
Penalizes variance accumulation from delaying execution, with $\lambda$ as the (adaptive) risk aversion parameter.

\subsubsection{Term 3: Transaction Cost}
\begin{equation}
C_3 = \sum_{t,v,k} c_v \cdot q_k \cdot x_{t,v,k}
\end{equation}
where $c_v$ is the per-share fee at venue $v$, encoding the linear cost on the QUBO diagonal.

\subsubsection{Term 4: Adverse Selection Cost}
\begin{equation}
C_4 = \sum_{t,v,k} \hat{\lambda}_K \cdot \text{VPIN}_t \cdot q_k^2 \cdot x_{t,v,k}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{\lambda}_K$ is Kyle's lambda estimated from recent order flow and $\text{VPIN}_t$ is the real-time toxicity measure. This term---absent from all prior QUBO execution formulations---penalizes execution during periods of high informed trading activity.

\subsubsection{Term 5: Inventory Risk Cost}
\begin{equation}
C_5 = \phi \sum_{t} \left(X - \sum_{\tau \leq t} \sum_{v,k} q_k x_{\tau,v,k}\right)^2
\end{equation}
Penalizes holding residual inventory, where $\phi$ is the inventory penalty.

\subsubsection{Term 6: Information Leakage Cost}
\begin{equation}
C_6 = \psi \sum_{t} \sum_{v_1 < v_2} \left(\sum_k q_k x_{t,v_1,k}\right) \left(\sum_k q_k x_{t,v_2,k}\right)
\end{equation}
Penalizes simultaneous execution across multiple venues within the same time slice, which signals large institutional orders and invites predatory strategies.

\subsection{Constraints}
We enforce two hard constraints via quadratic penalties:

\textbf{Equality constraint} (total shares):
\begin{equation}
P_{\text{eq}} \left(\sum_{t,v,k} q_k x_{t,v,k} - X\right)^2
\end{equation}

\textbf{Capacity constraint} (one level per slot):
\begin{equation}
P_{\text{cap}} \sum_{t,v} \left(\sum_k x_{t,v,k} - 1\right)^2
\end{equation}

\subsection{QUBO Matrix Construction}
The full QUBO matrix $Q$ encodes all six cost terms and penalty terms. The diagonal elements $Q_{ii}$ capture linear costs (transaction fees) and self-interaction terms. Off-diagonal elements $Q_{ij}$ encode quadratic interactions (impact, timing risk, leakage). The matrix has dimension $n \times n$ where $n = T \times V \times K$, with sparsity pattern determined by the interaction structure (Fig.~\ref{fig:qubo_matrix}).

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig02_qubo_matrix_structure.pdf}
\caption{QUBO matrix structure for a 5-slice, 1-venue, 4-level execution problem. Left: absolute values showing cost concentration on diagonal and near-diagonal blocks. Right: sparsity pattern revealing the block structure from time-venue-level indexing.}
\label{fig:qubo_matrix}
\end{figure}

\subsection{QUBO-to-Ising Conversion}
For QAOA execution on quantum hardware, we convert the QUBO to an Ising Hamiltonian via $x_i = (1 - z_i)/2$:
\begin{equation}
H = \sum_i h_i Z_i + \sum_{i<j} J_{ij} Z_i Z_j + \text{offset}
\end{equation}
where $h_i$ and $J_{ij}$ are derived from the QUBO matrix elements. The conversion is verified to be exact within machine precision (Fig.~\ref{fig:ising_conversion}).

\begin{figure}[t]

\end{figure}

\section{Market Microstructure Integration}
\label{sec:microstructure}

A key novelty of our framework is the real-time integration of market microstructure signals into the QUBO formulation.

\subsection{Kyle's Lambda Estimation}
We estimate the price impact coefficient using a rolling-window regression of price changes on signed order flow:
\begin{equation}
\Delta p_t = \hat{\lambda}_K \cdot \text{SignedVolume}_t + \epsilon_t
\end{equation}
The estimator updates at each tick, providing a dynamic measure of market depth (Fig.~\ref{fig:kyle_lambda}).

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig10_kyle_lambda.pdf}
\caption{Kyle's lambda estimation across volatility regimes. Top: price series with high-volatility regime (ticks 200--400). Bottom: estimated $\hat{\lambda}_K$ increases during the high-volatility period, reflecting reduced market depth.}
\label{fig:kyle_lambda}
\end{figure}

\subsection{VPIN: Order Flow Toxicity}
The Volume-Synchronized Probability of Informed Trading~\cite{easley2012flow} is computed using volume buckets:
\begin{equation}
\text{VPIN} = \frac{\sum_{n=1}^{N} |V_n^B - V_n^S|}{\sum_{n=1}^{N} (V_n^B + V_n^S)}
\end{equation}
where $V_n^B$ and $V_n^S$ are buy and sell volumes in bucket $n$. When $\text{VPIN} > 0.7$, the adverse selection cost term $C_4$ is amplified (Fig.~\ref{fig:vpin}).

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig11_vpin_estimation.pdf}
\caption{VPIN estimation with toxicity threshold at 0.7. Shaded red regions indicate periods where VPIN exceeds the threshold, triggering increased adverse selection penalties in the QUBO.}
\label{fig:vpin}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Adverse Selection Model}
The adverse selection cost combines effective spread and realized spread:
\begin{equation}
\text{AS} = \text{EffectiveSpread} - \text{RealizedSpread}
\end{equation}
This measures the information content of trades and feeds directly into the QUBO cost term $C_4$.

Fig.~\ref{fig:micro_dashboard} shows the integrated microstructure state across all four signals, demonstrating coordinated response to volatility regime changes.

\end{figure}

\section{Adaptive Risk Aversion}
\label{sec:adaptive_risk}

\subsection{Dual-Timescale EWMA Regime Detection}
We detect volatility regimes using two EWMA estimators with different decay rates:
\begin{align}
\hat{\sigma}_{\text{fast}}^2 &\leftarrow \alpha_f r_t^2 + (1-\alpha_f)\hat{\sigma}_{\text{fast}}^2, \quad \alpha_f = 0.06 \\
\hat{\sigma}_{\text{slow}}^2 &\leftarrow \alpha_s r_t^2 + (1-\alpha_s)\hat{\sigma}_{\text{slow}}^2, \quad \alpha_s = 0.01
\end{align}

The regime is classified by the ratio $R = \hat{\sigma}_{\text{fast}} / \hat{\sigma}_{\text{slow}}$:
\begin{equation}
\text{Regime} = \begin{cases}
\text{LOW} & R < 0.7 \\
\text{NORMAL} & 0.7 \leq R < 1.3 \\
\text{HIGH} & 1.3 \leq R < 2.0 \\
\text{EXTREME} & R \geq 2.0
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Dynamic Lambda Adjustment}
The risk aversion parameter is updated as:
\begin{equation}
\lambda_t = \lambda_{\text{base}} \times m(R_t)
\end{equation}
where $m(\cdot)$ maps regimes to multipliers: $m_{\text{LOW}} = 0.5$, $m_{\text{NORMAL}} = 1.0$, $m_{\text{HIGH}} = 2.0$, $m_{\text{EXTREME}} = 4.0$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:lambda_adaptation}).

\section{Latency-Decoupled Architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}

\subsection{System Design}
The architecture separates execution into two paths (Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}):

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Fast Path} ($<1$\,ms): Receives market data ticks, updates microstructure state, queries the policy queue, and dispatches execution orders.
\item \textbf{Slow Path} (100\,ms--5\,s): Builds QUBO matrices from current microstructure state, solves via QAOA or simulated annealing, and publishes new execution policies.
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig01_system_architecture.pdf}
\caption{Hybrid quantum-classical execution architecture with latency-decoupled fast and slow paths connected via an asynchronous policy queue.}
\label{fig:architecture}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Policy Queue and Staleness Bound}
The policy queue enables non-blocking communication between paths. When a new optimized policy arrives, the fast path atomically swaps it in. The staleness---time since the policy was computed---introduces a regret bounded by:
\begin{equation}
\text{Regret} \leq O\left(\sigma \sqrt{T_{\text{solve}}}\right)
\end{equation}
where $T_{\text{solve}}$ is the optimization solve time. For typical parameters ($\sigma = 0.002$, $T_{\text{solve}} = 500$\,ms), this yields $<2$\,bps of additional cost (Fig.~\ref{fig:staleness}).

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig25_policy_staleness.pdf}
\caption{Left: policy staleness over time, showing periodic updates from the slow path optimizer. Right: theoretical regret bound $O(\sigma\sqrt{T_{\text{solve}}})$ confirming sub-basis-point cost for solve times under 1s.}
\label{fig:staleness}
\end{figure}

\section{Quantum and Classical Solvers}
\label{sec:solvers}

\subsection{QAOA Implementation}
We implement QAOA with $p$ layers, each containing a cost unitary $U_C(\gamma) = e^{-i\gamma H_C}$ and mixer unitary $U_M(\beta) = e^{-i\beta \sum_i X_i}$:
\begin{equation}
|\boldsymbol{\gamma}, \boldsymbol{\beta}\rangle = \prod_{l=1}^{p} U_M(\beta_l) U_C(\gamma_l) |+\rangle^{\otimes n}
\end{equation}

The parameters $(\boldsymbol{\gamma}, \boldsymbol{\beta})$ are optimized via COBYLA to minimize $\langle \boldsymbol{\gamma}, \boldsymbol{\beta} | H_C | \boldsymbol{\gamma}, \boldsymbol{\beta} \rangle$. Circuit depth scales as $O(p \cdot n^2)$ for dense QUBO matrices (Fig.~\ref{fig:circuit_depth}).

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering

\end{figure}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Current Limitations and Classical Advantage}
At current problem scales (4--12 qubits), classical simulated annealing matches or exceeds QAOA performance. This is expected: near-term quantum devices operate in the NISQ regime where noise limits circuit depth. Our architecture is designed for the transition period---using SA as the production solver while maintaining quantum-readiness.

\subsection{Path to Quantum Advantage}
Quantum advantage for execution QUBO problems requires: (i) error rates below $10^{-4}$ for $>20$-qubit problems, (ii) sufficient connectivity for the dense Ising coupling structure, and (iii) fast enough classical-quantum communication for the latency-decoupled architecture. Based on IBM's roadmap (1000+ qubit processors with improved error rates), we project this crossover around 2028--2029 for production-scale execution problems ($>100$ QUBO variables).

\subsection{Practical Deployment Considerations}
The latency-decoupled architecture enables immediate production deployment with classical solvers while providing a clean integration point for quantum hardware as it matures. The policy queue abstraction means the execution engine is agnostic to the solver backend.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented a hybrid quantum-classical optimal execution engine that uniquely combines microstructure-aware QUBO formulation, adaptive risk management, and latency-decoupled architecture. Our six-term HFT QUBO cost function is the first to encode Kyle's lambda, VPIN, adverse selection, and information leakage costs into a quantum-compatible optimization objective. Walk-forward backtesting demonstrates 15--40\% implementation shortfall reduction versus baseline strategies. While current quantum hardware does not yet provide advantage over classical solvers at production scale, our architecture is quantum-ready and benchmarked on IBM hardware. The framework provides a complete, production-oriented system for the emerging intersection of quantum computing and high-frequency trading.

\subsection{Future Work}
Future directions include: (i) scaling to $>50$ qubit problems with error-corrected hardware, (ii) incorporating reinforcement learning for parameter adaptation, (iii) extending to options execution and multi-asset portfolios, and (iv) real-time deployment with co-located quantum processors.
